@mention cross-references — two ways to render the chip

The model we agreed: two referencing gestures, not three. You either anchor a Note/Question/Mark to verses in the main passage, or you @mention another passage inside a note's content. The standalone “Cross-reference” card goes away.

Gesture 1 · Anchor

Select verses in the passage → Note, Question, or Mark confusing. Lives against the main text.

Gesture 2 · @mention

Type @Malachi 4:5-6 in a note → a chip you can peek, open, and promote to a Support passage.

Gesture 3 · standalone Cross-reference card — retired; a reference to another passage is an @mention, not a thing anchored to the main text.

A

Chip strip beneath the note

The @ref stays as plain text you type in the box; detected references surface as chips in a strip under it. Keeps the ordinary textarea — low-risk.

In the margin

B

Inline chips in the text

The @ref becomes a chip within the flowing text as you write. Richer, more “live” — but the note editor becomes contenteditable (heavier, more fragile).

Either way, “Promote” makes the same thing → a Support passage

The promoted @mention becomes a Support passage anchored to the note's verses. It prints in the participant handout (framed with a return-question) and in the leader's notes — the existing v1 support-passage model, unchanged.

Trade-offs at a glance

A · Chip strip
  • Reuses the current textarea — small, safe change.
  • Chips grouped in one place; easy to scan a note's refs.
  • Ships this iteration; inline (B) can follow later.
  • Chip sits a little apart from the words it belongs to.
B · Inline chips
  • Reads most naturally — the ref is where you wrote it.
  • Feels the most “finished”.
  • Needs a contenteditable editor: caret, paste, undo, mobile quirks.
  • More surface area to get wrong; bigger test burden.
