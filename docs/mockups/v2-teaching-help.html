05 · Theme & aim

The passage's spine

The one line the whole study serves. Guidance sits quietly under each field — always the [I] line; Tell me more opens the fuller reasoning. Flip Guidance → Brief (top right) to collapse to the [I] lines only.

i
The theme is what the passage says, in one sentence. Not its topic (prayer, suffering) but its point: what the passage actually claims. Fill in the frame: In this passage, [author] shows that ___.
A topic is a word; a theme is a sentence. “Suffering” is a topic. “Suffering does not mean God has abandoned his people” is a theme, because it makes a claim you could agree or disagree with. If your sentence would fit three other passages equally well, it is probably a topic or a truism rather than this passage's theme, so push it until it needs this text.
i
The aim is what the author wanted to happen: not what the passage says, but what he wrote it to do to his first readers. Fill in the frame: He wrote it so that his readers would ___.
Theme and aim are a pair, and they are different. The theme is the point; the aim is the effect, the change the author wanted in the people who first received it. A passage written to reassure frightened believers is doing something different from one written to warn complacent ones, even when the theme sounds similar, and the aim is what tells them apart.
Faithfulness is not certainty

Faithfulness is not the same as certainty. Several themes could fairly be defended from this passage, and landing among them is faithful, even if another careful reader would choose slightly differently. ▾ more

i
Say in one sentence how this passage gets to Christ. Follow the route rather than jumping straight there: text → its place in the Bible's unfolding story → its fulfilment in Christ → us, who are in him.
The route matters more than the destination. Do not reach for Christ by resemblance, and do not tack the cross onto the end. Trace the line the passage itself sits on: a promise, a covenant, an office, an institution, an unsolved problem. Follow that line forward to him, and then to us in him. — after Goldsworthy
