This tool refuses to write your study for you, and that refusal is the point. A tool that hands you questions produces someone who cannot write questions; a tool that hands you a theme produces someone who never learned to find one. The work is slow on purpose. Preparing a study is how you come to understand a passage well enough to lead others into it, and there is no shortcut that leaves that understanding behind. So the tool structures the work, prompts you, and checks what you have written, but the thinking stays yours. The struggle is not in the way of the training; it is the training.

It is built for more than one use: preparing your own study, learning the craft for the first time, or training someone else by watching how they work through it. A leader who has done this a few times will move quickly; someone doing it for the first time will lean on the guidance at every step. Both are the point.

There is an honest objection to a tool like this, and it is worth facing rather than hiding. Graeme Goldsworthy names inductive study guides, the kind that hand you a passage and a set of prescribed questions, as a prime source of a real problem. Not because the method is wrong, but because on its own it is not enough: it is good as far as it goes, yet it either assumes people can already see how a passage fits into the whole of Scripture, and so how it relates to Christ, or it ignores the need to do so. That criticism lands on exactly this kind of tool. The answer is not to route around it but to build the missing step in. It is why the theme and aim, and the question of how the passage gets to Christ, are not optional extras here but phases you work through. A study that stops at careful observation and sensible application, without ever asking how this text speaks of Christ, is the very thing he warns against. *— after Graeme Goldsworthy, Preaching the Whole Bible as Christian Scripture (Eerdmans, 2000), ch. 1*
